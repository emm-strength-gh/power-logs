Bar velocity, m/s

    Settings

    Velocity anchors

    The speed of a true grinding single is personal but stable. Set it per lift and every RPE shifts with it.

    Stalls

    A stall is the bar almost stopping mid-lift (between 15% and 90% of the way up) for 0.3 s or more — 0.4 s on deadlifts. Setup at the floor and the lockout hold never count. Tap a rep's hitch tag to un-count it.

    “Only if slow”: a stalled rep is RPE 10 when its speed already reads 8.5 or harder; on a faster rep the stall adds one RPE instead.

    Getting numbers you can trust

    • Phone on a tripod or propped against something solid. Handheld camera motion goes straight into the velocity trace.
    • Stand perpendicular to the bar, lens roughly level with the middle of the range of motion. Shooting from an angle or from the floor foreshortens the bar path and reads low.
    • Film the same bar end throughout, and scale against a plate in that same plane.
    • For tape mode use strongly coloured tape — fluoro pink, orange or green. White, black, silver and grey have no hue to lock onto.
    • For area mode, pick a patch with real texture or contrast in it — a collar edge, engraved lettering, a knurling band — not a smooth painted surface. A smaller patch locks on tighter but loses it more easily if the bar drifts out of the search window; a larger one is more forgiving but slower to match.
    • 30 fps gives a usable mean velocity but a noisy peak. 60 fps is comfortable, 120 or 240 better.
    • Expect mean concentric velocity within roughly 0.03 m/s of a linear transducer with a good setup, and tighter at slow grinding speeds. RPE carries about ±1 until you set your own anchor.
    • Sumo and conventional hit the same bar speed at a genuine max, but sumo is slower at every effort below it and travels 20–25% less distance, so each stance gets its own table. A lifter's anchor for one stance does not transfer to the other — set both separately.
    • Deadlift velocity is less reliable than squat or bench, and load–velocity profiles tend to under-predict a real deadlift max. Trust velocity loss across a set more than the absolute number here.
